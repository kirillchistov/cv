## Cases

### 1. An occasion in the basket
**Category:** B2C · FMCG Food
**Profile:** A national dairy producer, mid-price, sold mainly in modern grocery chains.
**Period:** March–August

1) **Problem.** Several almost identical lines sit on the shelf. A discount in the chain's leaflet lifts units for a week and teaches the shopper to wait for a coupon. The brand needs a repeat purchase without a permanent promo depth.
2) **Solution and result.** The same SKU was filmed in three everyday worlds: breakfast with children, a snack after training, a late dinner. In the chain's app a person sees the world that matched their basket over 90 days. Incremental sales in test stores **+6.4%** versus the geo-holdout, repeat purchase within 28 days **+9 p.p.**, placement ROMI **310%** (incremental gross profit over retail-media cost).
3) **Creative and tools.** The creative sells the occasion; the pack and the taste stay recognizable. A QR on the cup opens the recipe for that occasion, not a generic brand page. The leaflet promo turns on only for the segment that already has the occasion in the basket. Bundle: in-app retail media, segments by basket contents, QR, promo leaflet.
4) **How it was measured.** 15% of stores of a comparable format were held out with no personalized creative. Brand sales and repeat were compared in the chain's loyalty card.

### 2. Dinner by half past seven
**Category:** B2C · FMCG Food
**Profile:** A producer of frozen ready meals, focused on large cities, sold through chains and a small own delivery site.
**Period:** October–February

1) **Problem.** The evening decision takes a minute: the person picks delivery of ready food, and frozen food loses because the ad looks like "stock up", not like dinner tonight.
2) **Solution and result.** In a minute the bot assembles dinner from what the person says is at home and adds one or two of the brand's SKUs. Site conversion to order **1.8% → 3.1%**, D2C average order **+14%**, redemption of the Telegram code **22%**.
3) **Creative and tools.** The creative is a card for a specific dinner with the minutes on the pan, not a banner of "−25% on mains". The same dinner goes out as a geo-push from the chain's app between 17:00 and 18:30 near office clusters. On the site the bundle is already in the cart. Bundle: Telegram bot, retailer geo-push, D2C bundle feed.
4) **How it was measured.** The bot code was joined to the site order. The geo-push was compared with the chain's ordinary evening banner in the same zones, week on week.

### 3. The insert people forward
**Category:** B2C · FMCG Food
**Profile:** A producer of chocolate sets, a strong New Year peak, on shelf in chains and on marketplaces.
**Period:** November–January

1) **Problem.** A gift set is chosen two days before the holiday, and on the banner every box looks the same. Creator reach is expensive, and a "what's inside" review is the same as last year.
2) **Solution and result.** The giver writes a name and one sentence on a landing page; the layout builds an insert that goes into the marketplace order against a personal code. Sell-through of gift SKUs **+37%** versus the previous season at comparable distribution, brand share of "gift set" queries **+4.2 p.p.**, organic reach of the inserts **2.4 million** with no extra seeding spend.
3) **Creative and tools.** Creators are given other people's notes, not a tasting: the creative is someone else's sentence large and the box small. On the marketplace the product carries a "with a note" badge. The factory does not move to one-off box printing: only the insert is personal. Bundle: landing page, generative layout, unique creator codes, marketplace card.
4) **How it was measured.** Season on season, adjusted for store and item count. Search covered brand and category queries. Reach was counted from public tagged posts, with no contest inflation.

### 4. A weaning calendar
**Category:** B2C · FMCG Food
**Profile:** A producer of purées and cereals. The buyer is a parent of a child under three. Channels are grocery chains and pharmacies.
**Period:** 12 months

1) **Problem.** The trial SKU is bought once "on advice". Then the parent gets lost in the age for introducing foods and moves to private label or to the neighboring brand that simply appears more often.
2) **Solution and result.** Communication is tied to the child's month, which the parent entered. Two weeks before a typical expansion of the diet, a note arrives on what is now appropriate and which SKU is the logical next step. Trial to repeat within 60 days **29% → 46%**, share of category spend among participants **+8 p.p.**, unsubscribes below **1.5%**.
3) **Creative and tools.** The tone is a plain calendar, with no promises of development and no comparison with "ordinary food". The coupon in the chain's loyalty card opens only for the next calendar step, not for the whole line. The pharmacist has a card with the same three points. Bundle: messenger scenario, retailer coupon, pharmacy materials.
4) **How it was measured.** The cohort that registered the child's month versus those who bought the trial and did not register. Repeat was looked up in partner chains' loyalty cards.

### 5. Three situations, one product
**Category:** B2C · FMCG non-food
**Profile:** A home-cleaning brand with a wide line. The main competition is the chains' private label.
**Period:** April–September

1) **Problem.** A "clean kitchen" film misses equally the homes with few things, small children, or a dog. Private label takes the shelf on price because the brand never explains why this apartment should pay more.
2) **Solution and result.** Three creative households: "few things", "children and mess", "a home with animals". The people, the mess in frame, and the argument change: time, formula, hair. Household penetration in test cities **+1.6 p.p.**, coupon redemption **16%** versus **5%** for the mass mailing, share of next baskets switching from private label **+11%** among those who saw "their" household.
3) **Creative and tools.** DOOH near residential blocks plays the household that matched the district profile in the retailer's data. The same week the chain's app carries a coupon and a promo mechanic for the segment: a family pack, a large format for animals, a small format for "few things". Bundle: DOOH, retail media, basket segments (baby goods, pet food), different shelf mechanics.
4) **How it was measured.** Test cities versus cities with ordinary shelf support. Switching was read from the loyalty card: private label in the previous basket, the brand in the next.

### 6. A 14-day ritual
**Category:** B2C · FMCG non-food
**Profile:** A mass shampoo and mask brand, national distribution, more than ten "for your hair type" promises on the shelf.
**Period:** February–June

1) **Problem.** The shopper knows their hair type only vaguely and sees the whole line at once in advertising. The click is cheap, the first purchase is accidental, and a second bottle often never happens.
2) **Solution and result.** A five-question quiz ends on one bottle and a 14-day ritual. Every later touch shows that bottle and that ritual. Dynamic-ad CTR **+46%** versus a static line banner, new brand buyers **+17%**, CPA **−24%**, repeat within 90 days **+8 p.p.**
3) **Creative and tools.** The ad creative is built from the quiz answer: the person, the texture, one ritual sentence. Samples in gyms go only to the segment that chose "I wash it after training". Emails on day 3 and day 10 ask about the hair, not about a discount on the second bottle. Bundle: quiz, dynamic creative on VK and in programmatic, sampling, two trigger emails.
4) **How it was measured.** A/B: dynamic creative with the quiz character versus a banner of the whole line, on the same audiences. Repeat was read from the loyalty card and from a repeat marketplace order with the quiz code.

### 7. The first cold day
**Category:** B2C · Pharma
**Profile:** A producer of OTC remedies for cold symptoms, sold through pharmacy chains.
**Period:** September–March

1) **Problem.** The seasonal campaign starts by the calendar, while the cold arrives in different weeks in different cities. By then the shelf is tired of frightening "don't let the illness start" creatives, and the pharmacist speaks differently from the screen.
2) **Solution and result.** Communication in a city turns on the first day the temperature falls below that city's threshold. The creative is an ordinary morning when the voice goes before a call, and one plain OTC step. Sales in test cities **+13%** versus control at a comparable stock, pharmacist recommendation **+7 p.p.**, brand queries in the pharmacy's internal search **+19%**.
3) **Creative and tools.** In the chain's app the banner and the search hint use the same words as the card at the counter. Search follows the everyday situation, with no diagnosis and no promise to cure. Bundle: city weather trigger, pharmacy in-app, pharmacist materials, internal search.
4) **How it was measured.** Pairs of cities with a similar pharmacy network and last year's seasonality. Recommendation was checked by a mystery visit using the same everyday wording.

### 8. The course as a series
**Category:** B2C · Pharma
**Profile:** A mass vitamin-complex brand. The pack is sized for 30 days. Sold in pharmacies and on marketplaces.
**Period:** January–April

1) **Problem.** After the holidays the course is bought with the intention to "start", and dropped in the second week. The next jar does not sell, and reviews pile up as "I felt nothing, I quit".
2) **Solution and result.** The course is laid out as four episodes. An episode opens at the hour the person usually replies in a messenger, and it talks about the goal they chose: after winter, for a race, for exams. Completion among those who registered the pack **24% → 43%**, repeat purchase of the course **+28%**, share of "quit in the second week" reviews **−35%**.
3) **Creative and tools.** This is not a "time to take it" reminder. An episode is a short everyday scene about sleep, food, or training, and the course's place in it, with no therapeutic promises. Someone who bought on a marketplace and did not open the second episode gets one email with that episode, not a discount on another SKU. Bundle: on-pack code, messenger, marketplace mailing, a test of send time.
4) **How it was measured.** Those who registered the code versus buyers of the same pack who did not register, a 45-day window. Review tone was coded from a hand-labeled sample.

### 9. Three steps instead of a shelf
**Category:** B2C · Pharma
**Profile:** A pharmacy skincare brand, a high price, about forty SKUs. The choice most often ends on one jar picked at random.
**Period:** nine months

1) **Problem.** The consultant and the site list the line. The shopper takes one cream, the routine never forms, and a month later some purchases come back: "I didn't understand what to combine it with".
2) **Solution and result.** A quiz on self-described skin state assembles a morning and an evening of three steps. Average SKUs in the routine **1.4 → 2.6**, quiz-to-purchase conversion **12%**, returns **−19%**, shelf revenue in test pharmacies **+11%**.
3) **Creative and tools.** The quiz result is one diagram card, and the same card is printed with the purchase. On day 14 a question arrives about which step did not fit, and one step is replaced. The copy contains no diagnoses and no promise to "remove the problem". The pharmacist works from the same diagram as the screen. Bundle: quiz on the site and in the pharmacy app, floor script, day-14 trigger.
4) **How it was measured.** Pharmacies with the diagram versus pharmacies in the same chain without it. Returns came from loyalty-card receipts. Quiz conversion was counted from quiz completion to a receipt within 7 days.

### 10. A conversation about fit
**Category:** B2C · Fashion
**Profile:** A mid-market women's clothing brand, its own web store and about 40 shops. The main hole in the economics is size returns.
**Period:** August–February

1) **Problem.** The lookbook sells an image on a model. The shopper orders two sizes "just in case" and returns one. The abandoned-cart email finishes with a promo code and increases those same returns.
2) **Solution and result.** After clicks on the size chart, the product page switches to how the style fits a figure close to the stated measurements, and to a free 15-minute fitting: the size is already held at the nearest shop. Return rate **36% → 27%**, product-page conversion **+0.9 p.p.**, email revenue **+34%**, share of orders with a booked fitting **+12 p.p.**
3) **Creative and tools.** The cart email says how this style sits at the waist and in length, and offers a slot. The promo code is removed from that email. In the shop the hold opens on the same size that was viewed online. Bundle: fit content on the product page, store booking, CRM trigger, hold at the shop.
4) **How it was measured.** "Size" returns before and after, on the same assortment. The fit email was tested against an email with a promo-code deadline, attribution window 72 hours.

### 11. The next size
**Category:** B2C · Fashion
**Profile:** A children's clothing brand for ages 2–10, orders from its own site and from marketplaces. The payer is the parent.
**Period:** a school year

1) **Problem.** The child outgrows a garment faster than the parent returns to the app. Seasonal promo mailings open poorly: they are not about this child. "Too small" and "too big" returns eat the marketplace margin.
2) **Solution and result.** Ten to fourteen weeks after the purchase the parent receives one card: which items of this age usually become small, and which next size is logical. Repeat within 120 days **+26%**, size returns **−23%**, open rate of size emails **44%** versus **18%** for seasonal promos.
3) **Creative and tools.** In August the card looks like getting ready for school, in December like a holiday; the logic is the same. The "time for a size" segment is excluded from discount mailings so a discount does not replace the occasion. On the marketplace the same size rules are placed in the Q&A on the product card. Bundle: CRM by order date and age, promo suppression, marketplace card content.
4) **How it was measured.** Opens and repeat were compared with promo emails to the same base in neighboring weeks. Return reasons came from the marketplace cabinet.

### 12. A scene, not specifications
**Category:** B2C · Electronics
**Profile:** A national web store of electronics and home appliances with pickup points, a wide range and a long cart.
**Period:** November

1) **Problem.** In November a person compares ten product pages of hertz and watts and abandons the cart. A "you forgot an item" email returns them to the table and to a cheaper competitor.
2) **Solution and result.** The browse path is gathered into one of three scenes: remote work, study, play. The cart and the ads show the assembled scene — desk, light, sound — and a one-line trade-in of the old device. Average order through the configurator **₽38,400** versus **₽24,900** for the control email of specifications, CRM-trigger revenue **+42%**, share of orders with a trade-in **+6 p.p.**
3) **Creative and tools.** Specifications stay behind a spoiler. Dynamic retargeting shows the same scene, not a single SKU with a discount badge. Bundle: scene rules from browsing, dynamic retargeting, cart trigger, a trade-in calculator in the same email.
4) **How it was measured.** A/B of abandoned-cart emails. Significance was checked on order value and on the trade-in share. Post-click window: 5 days.

### 13. The month in plain words
**Category:** B2C · Banking
**Profile:** A retail bank from the top of the market, focused on existing payroll-project clients.
**Period:** two quarters

1) **Problem.** The client is offered products several times a month. They stop opening messages, and they pick cashback categories by default and do not change behavior.
2) **Solution and result.** Once a month the client receives a short summary of spending in ordinary language and one cashback category that explains that summary. Turnover in the target categories **+8.5%** versus the control group, penetration of category choice **11% → 27%**, complaints about mailings **−31%**.
3) **Creative and tools.** The tone is taken from support correspondence: drier or more conversational. Those who have not opened a message for three months do not receive the summary. In the branch the cashback script matches the text the person has already seen. Bundle: CVM on spend categories, app inbox, suppression by fatigue, branch script.
4) **How it was measured.** A 10% holdout of the base with no summary. Turnover was compared inside the categories the summary named. Complaints were contacts tagged as communications.

### 14. A card for a large occasion
**Category:** B2C · Banking
**Profile:** A retail bank. The product is a credit card with a grace period. Applications come from the base and from the site.
**Period:** May–October

1) **Problem.** The card campaign runs on the marketing calendar. The person sees a rate at a moment when there is no large purchase, refuses, and gets irritated. By the fourth message conversion falls, and the risk profile of those who still applied head-on is worse.
2) **Solution and result.** The message goes out when behavior shows a large occasion: a trip, a renovation, medical spend. The frame is the occasion and the grace period on it. Application to issued card **17% → 26%**, limit utilization in the first 45 days **+9 p.p.**, cost per approved card **−18%**. 30+ delinquency in the fourth month did not rise versus the previous portfolio.
3) **Creative and tools.** After two refusals the bank stays quiet until a new occasion. A call is allowed only for the segment with a high probability of issuance and a low share of previous complaints. Bundle: intent triggers, an in-app screen, the site, a call, one frequency cap. The scoring threshold was not lowered.
4) **How it was measured.** A holdout with no occasion trigger. Utilization and early delinquency were watched alongside, so a rise in issuance would not turn out to be a rise in a bad portfolio.

### 15. The savings pot as a story
**Category:** B2C · Banking
**Profile:** A bank with a strong mobile app. The product is goals and savings pots. The core audience is active clients aged 18–35.
**Period:** January–June

1) **Problem.** A pot is opened out of curiosity and abandoned: a push about the balance looks like a statement. In the same weeks the bank offers a deposit, and the goal falls apart.
2) **Solution and result.** The goal is named in the client's words: a trip, a cushion of three rents, a bicycle. Once a week, one scene of progress. For a cushion the tone is calm; for a trip the frame is the city and the date. Share of active clients with at least one goal **7% → 18%**, average balance on goals **+37%** over four months, goal retention on day 90 **49%**.
3) **Creative and tools.** In the weeks of the story, product pushes about deposits are off for this segment. In-app stories and the push use the same goal name. Bundle: goal onboarding, in-app stories, pushes by goal type, suppression of neighboring campaigns.
4) **How it was measured.** A control without stories on comparable active clients. Balance and retention were counted on goal accounts, not on all of the client's savings.

### 16. A year with the policy
**Category:** B2C · Insurance
**Profile:** A multiline insurer with a large voluntary motor portfolio. Renewal runs through agents and the client account.
**Period:** a renewal cycle

1) **Problem.** The renewal email opens on the price. The client takes the quote into a comparison and bargains. The agent gets a script of "the tariff is about to rise" and says the same thing as the email, only louder.
2) **Solution and result.** First the client sees their year: claim-free status, which options were not used, which one option is cheaper given their mileage. The price opens on the second screen. Renewal **62% → 71%**, share of renewals with no call **+15 p.p.**, complaints of "they started with the price" **−22%**. The loss ratio of this portfolio slice stayed at last year's level.
3) **Creative and tools.** The agent sees in the CRM the same one-page story the client sees in the account, and starts the conversation there. Bundle: client account, email, agent card. The digital door and the agent door say the same thing.
4) **How it was measured.** A/B on the renewal flow: price on the first screen versus the story on the first. Loss ratio was checked so the renewal gain was not carried by contracts that were already loss-making.

### 17. Two editions of one week
**Category:** B2C · Investments
**Profile:** A retail broker with an app. Advertising brings many new accounts; a minority get funded.
**Period:** September–March

1) **Problem.** In the first week a beginner is hit with products and "ideas". They cannot tell education from a sale, and either do not fund the account or fund it and write to support.
2) **Solution and result.** "Watchers" — those who opened securities and did not buy — receive a read of one security from their list and an explicit risk frame. "Traders" receive a retelling of their own week. Share of accounts funded within 14 days **+21%**, retention of funded accounts on day 90 **+9 p.p.**, contacts of "what did you sell me" **−27%**.
3) **Creative and tools.** Both formats are one page with no promise of return and no "act now". Product pushes are off for the first 14 days. Ads to similar audiences use the same reads, not a market chart. Each template passes compliance. Bundle: a segment by in-app actions, in-app, two email templates, a pause of product campaigns.
4) **How it was measured.** Cohorts of new accounts before and after the change of the first week, plus a holdout on the old pushes. Contacts were coded by topic in support.

### 18. A note from the place
**Category:** B2C · Travel
**Profile:** A package-holiday tour operator, a mid-range check, families and couples. Sales from the site and through agencies.
**Period:** January–April, early booking of summer

1) **Problem.** A catalog of two hundred hotels does not help a family with a child and a "no visa" constraint. The request is postponed, and the agent in the office starts the search over and loses the person who had already been looking.
2) **Solution and result.** From past trips and four filters, three routes are assembled. Each is written as a short guide's note and respects the constraint the person set. Conversion to a request **0.9% → 2.1%**, share of requests that reached a contract **+7 p.p.**, time from first visit to request shorter by **4 days**.
3) **Creative and tools.** Retargeting plays a frame from the note the person opened, not a generic beach of the destination. The agency CRM holds the same three routes. Bundle: a quiz for new visitors, a configurator for returning ones, dynamic retargeting, a card in the agent's CRM.
4) **How it was measured.** The site funnel was compared with the previous early-booking window. Request quality was read from the share of contracts, not only from the number of forms.

### 19. Why go on a Tuesday
**Category:** B2C · Travel
**Profile:** A domestic ticket service with a large base for email and push. Midweek flight load regularly sags.
**Period:** a year, a pilot on 40 routes

1) **Problem.** An email of "−20% on every route" lifts unsubscribes and sends people onto Friday flights that are already full. The empty Tuesday stays empty.
2) **Solution and result.** An email is assembled only if the flight has seats and the person has a reason in that city: food, a trail, theatre, relatives. One city, one date, one reason. Load on target flights **+2.8 p.p.** versus control routes, share of ancillaries in the order **+12%**, unsubscribes **−19%** versus promotional mailings.
3) **Creative and tools.** At the bottom of the email, one partner block — a restaurant or a museum for the same reason. A city with no reason gets no email, even if the flight is empty. Bundle: a trigger on load and interest, email, app push, a partner block.
4) **How it was measured.** Pilot routes versus routes similar in distance and day of week with no emails. Unsubscribes were compared with the regular promotion on the same base.

### 20. A route sheet for the test drive
**Category:** B2C · Automotive
**Profile:** A mass-market brand, sales through dealers in large cities. Aggregator leads are expensive, and test-drive show-up is low.
**Period:** March–November

1) **Problem.** The ad talks about power and trims. The person leaves a request and does not come: at the showroom an abstract loop around the dealer's district is waiting.
2) **Solution and result.** The creative names the trim and a road near home: the school run, the winter road to the dacha, a city with nowhere convenient to park. A 15-second film opens on a frame of their city. Test-drive show-up **24% → 37%**, lead to contract **3.8% → 5.5%**, CPL **−15%**.
3) **Creative and tools.** The invitation offers this route and a slot, not "a convenient time". On the call the dealer repeats the route from the request. Bundle: dynamic creative with a city layer, a lead form with one question about the scenario, a booking in the dealer system, a call script.
4) **How it was measured.** Show-up and contracts were counted from dealer records before and after the invitation changed. Aggregator leads were separated from the brand's own.

### 21. This car's first winter
**Category:** B2C · Automotive
